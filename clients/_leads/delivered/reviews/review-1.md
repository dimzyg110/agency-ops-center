# Independent Review: 3 Partnership Strategy Plans for The Delivered Inc
**Reviewer:** Senior Business Reviewer, Roviq  
**Date:** 2026-02-10  
**Review Scope:** Plans 1, 2, and 3 for Delivered partnership proposal  

---

## Overall Assessment

These three plans construct an ambitious and largely coherent pitch for a strategic partnership with The Delivered Inc. The market opportunity (social equity delivery exclusivity until 2029, zero native app competitors) is genuinely compelling and well-articulated. However, the plans collectively suffer from **inflated revenue projections with weak justification**, **critical omissions around license renewal and MA advertising law**, **an unrealistic 8–10 week app timeline**, and **inconsistent deal terms that would confuse a sophisticated founder reading all three documents**. The plans read more like a pitch to Randy than a pitch to Delivered's founders — they assume the audience is already convinced that an app is the silver bullet, rather than making the case for why Delivered should trade equity for something they haven't asked for.

## Critical Issues (Must-Fix Before Presenting)

### 1. License Expiration Is NEVER Addressed
Delivered's license (MD1303) expires **3/5/2026** — roughly **3 weeks** from the date of this proposal. Not a single plan mentions this. This is an existential risk:
- If the license lapses, the entire partnership is worthless.
- If renewal is pending, the timeline for Phase 1 work changes.
- Any sophisticated founder will notice that we're proposing a multi-year partnership without acknowledging that their operating license is about to expire.
- **Fix:** Add a section acknowledging the renewal timeline, confirming Delivered's renewal status is a precondition to the partnership, and ideally offering to assist with any renewal-related compliance work.

### 2. Hacked Website — Mentioned But Underweighted
Plan 1 mentions the hacked site in Phase 1 and Section 2.5, which is good. But the urgency is undersold. The casino spam pages are **actively present in Google's index RIGHT NOW**. This isn't a "triage" item — it's a **5-alarm fire** that is:
- Destroying Delivered's domain authority daily
- Potentially exposing customer data
- Creating compliance liability (a cannabis licensee hosting gambling content)
- Making the founders look negligent to anyone who Googles them

Plans 2 and 3 don't mention the hack at all. **Fix:** The hack should be the OPENING of the pitch — "Your house is on fire. Let us help you put it out. Then let's talk about building." This creates immediate trust and demonstrates competence before asking for equity.

### 3. Revenue Projections Are Aspirational, Not Justified
Plan 1 projects $55K → $180K/month in 12 months (+227%). This is aggressive for a bootstrapped cannabis delivery operator expanding into competitive Boston territory while simultaneously building an app, launching exclusive products, and running programmatic SEO. Specific issues:

- **Baseline revenue ($55K/month) is a guess.** "Conservative mid-range estimate" based on dividing total market by 27 operators. This assumes even distribution. Delivered operates in Central MA (lower density, lower income, lower cannabis spend per capita than Boston). Their actual revenue could be $30K–40K/month. Presenting projections on a potentially inflated baseline destroys credibility if the founders know their actual numbers.
- **The 73% growth in 6 months is heroic.** $55K → $95K requires the app to be live by Month 4 (tight), Boston expansion by Month 6 (very tight), and SEO recovery to be meaningful within 3 months (possible but not guaranteed given the severity of the hack).
- **$180K/month by Month 12 requires 80+ municipalities.** Operating in 80+ towns requires drivers, inventory, and logistics infrastructure that isn't costed or planned in any of the three documents. The plans treat geographic expansion as "flip a switch" when it requires real-world operational capacity.
- **Fix:** Present three scenarios (conservative, base, optimistic). Use a conservative baseline ($40K/month) and show what the deal looks like even if growth is 50% of projections. This actually makes the pitch STRONGER — "Even if we're half right, the equity is worth it."

### 4. MA Cannabis Advertising Regulations — Completely Ignored
Massachusetts has **strict cannabis advertising regulations** under 935 CMR 500.105. Key restrictions:
- No advertising visible from a public way (limits outdoor/billboard — Plan 3 proposes billboards)
- No advertising that depicts consumption (limits lifestyle imagery — Plan 3's creative direction runs right into this)
- No advertising with "unsubstantiated claims about cannabis" (limits some of the effect-based marketing proposed)
- No advertising attractive to persons under 21 (limits playful tone, social media strategy)
- Digital advertising requires age-gating
- All ads must include health warnings

Plan 3 proposes billboards ("Just the logo, 'Cannabis, Delivered.', and a QR code"), social media content depicting lifestyle consumption, and street teams with flyers — all of which require compliance review that is never mentioned. **Fix:** Add a compliance overlay to Plan 3, noting which tactics are subject to 935 CMR 500.105 review and how the creative will be adapted accordingly.

### 5. Cannabis Payment Restrictions — Addressed in Plan 2, Ignored Elsewhere
Plan 2 correctly identifies that credit cards cannot be used (ACH, debit, cash only). Plans 1 and 3 don't address this at all. Plan 1 mentions "Apple Pay integration for frictionless checkout" as a benefit of native iOS — but **Apple Pay IS a credit card payment method and cannot be used for cannabis transactions.** This is a factual error that a cannabis operator will catch immediately and it will damage credibility. **Fix:** Remove the Apple Pay reference from Plan 1. Ensure all three plans consistently acknowledge the payment limitation and position the app's payment UX as solving the friction of ACH/debit/cash (which is a real problem worth solving).

### 6. The 8–10 Week App Timeline Is Not Credible
Plan 2 proposes an 8–10 week MVP build for:
- Full ordering system with real-time inventory sync
- Payment processing (ACH + debit + cash — each requiring separate integrations)
- ID verification and age-gating
- Real-time driver tracking with maps
- Loyalty/rewards system
- Push notification infrastructure
- Admin dashboard
- App Store submission (cannabis apps face extra Apple review scrutiny)

This is a **6–9 month project** for a solo developer, even an exceptional one. With a realistic scope cut to a true MVP (ordering + payment + basic tracking), 12–16 weeks is more honest. Apple's review process alone for a cannabis-adjacent app can take 2–4 weeks with rejections and resubmissions. **Fix:** Either dramatically reduce the MVP scope (V1 = ordering + compliance + basic tracking; loyalty/referrals/advanced features come in V2 at Month 5–6) or extend the timeline to 14–18 weeks. Being honest about timeline builds more trust than an aggressive promise you'll miss.

## Warnings (Should-Fix, Risk If Ignored)

### 7. Equity Ask May Be Too High for What's Demonstrated
The plans ask for 4–15% equity in exchange for an app that doesn't exist yet, SEO work that hasn't started, and supply chain introductions that haven't been formalized. From Delivered's perspective:
- They have a working business generating real revenue
- They've been operational for 2.5 years
- They survived without an app, SEO agency, or AC connection
- They're being asked to give equity to someone they barely know

**The pitch needs to answer: "Why can't we just hire a dev shop for $100K and keep 100% of our equity?"** The plans gesture at this (Randy's cannabis domain knowledge, AC relationship, ongoing growth engine) but never directly confront the objection. **Fix:** Add a "Why Not Just Hire a Dev Shop" section that honestly addresses this and makes the case for partnership > vendor relationship.

### 8. AC Wholesale Relationship Is Presented as Fact, Likely Isn't
The plans treat the AC supply chain as a done deal ("AC supplies wholesale directly to Delivered's warehouse"). Is there a signed agreement? Is pricing negotiated? Does AC have the capacity to supply a delivery operator expanding statewide? The plans present this as a core differentiator without any evidence it's been formalized. If Delivered's founders ask "Is AC committed to supplying us?" and the answer is "Well, we know them..." — the credibility of the entire vertical integration story collapses. **Fix:** Either formalize the AC relationship before presenting, or frame it honestly as "We have an existing relationship with AC and will facilitate an introduction and wholesale negotiation."

### 9. No Discussion of IP Ownership
What happens to the app if the partnership dissolves? Plan 1 has a brief mention of IP reversion (pre-cliff) and perpetual license, but this is buried and vague. For Delivered's founders, this is a dealbreaker question: "If we give you equity and you build the app, and then we part ways — who owns the app?" **Fix:** Create a clear, prominent IP section. The app should transfer to Delivered upon full vesting (or upon reaching specific milestones). If vesting isn't complete, Delivered should still have ownership of customer data and order history.

### 10. Social Equity Status — Mentioned But Not Leveraged Correctly
The plans mention social equity as a "moat" (regulatory protection). But they don't demonstrate understanding of what social equity MEANS to the founders personally. Ruben, Eddy, and Tut likely have lived experience with the War on Drugs. The proposal treats social equity as a business advantage rather than also acknowledging its personal and community significance. Plan 3 gets closest ("Amplify, Don't Exploit") but even there, it's presented as a marketing tactic. **Fix:** The tone should acknowledge the founders' journey and position Roviq as supporting their mission, not just capitalizing on their license status.

### 11. Plan 3 Is Incomplete
Plan 3 explicitly says "[Sections 3-8 to be completed in Part 2]" but then includes those sections anyway in a different style and depth. The document reads like it was assembled from two separate drafts. Sections 1–2 have a distinct, high-energy creative voice. Sections 3–8 shift to a more conventional marketing strategy format. This inconsistency suggests the plan wasn't reviewed before being finalized. **Fix:** Unify the voice and remove the "Part 2" reference. Either commit to the creative voice throughout or don't.

### 12. No Contingency If App Doesn't Get App Store Approval
Apple has rejected cannabis-related apps before. While delivery/ordering apps are generally permissible with proper age-gating, approval is not guaranteed. None of the plans discuss what happens if Apple rejects the app. **Fix:** Address this head-on. Note that the app will be designed to comply with Apple's guidelines (age-gating, no consumption imagery in screenshots, no cannabis leaf in the icon), and have a fallback plan (progressive web app as interim).

## Strengths (What's Genuinely Good)

### Plan 1
- **Market framing is excellent.** The "app gap" table, the comparison to food delivery penetration, and the social equity moat explanation are all compelling and well-structured.
- **Three deal options** is smart. Giving Delivered choices makes them feel in control rather than receiving an ultimatum.
- **Revenue share thresholds** in Option A ($75K floor) are well-designed — they align incentives and show confidence.
- **Risk mitigation section** is honest and addresses real concerns. This builds trust.
- **The "Why NOW" section** effectively creates urgency without being pushy.

### Plan 2
- **Payment compliance** is correctly handled (ACH, debit, cash — no credit cards). This shows cannabis industry knowledge.
- **Purchase limit enforcement** and compliance features demonstrate regulatory awareness.
- **Backend architecture section** gives just enough technical detail to be credible without overwhelming non-technical founders.
- **Cost comparison** (in-house vs. agency vs. dev team) is effective at framing the value proposition.

### Plan 3
- **"Kleenex of cannabis delivery" framing** is genuinely brilliant and memorable. This is the kind of vision that gets founders excited.
- **Brand voice examples** ("Don't Say This / Say This Instead") are concrete and immediately demonstrate creative capability.
- **Multilingual play** is a smart, differentiated insight. Central MA has significant Spanish and Portuguese-speaking populations, and no cannabis company is targeting them.
- **Go-to-market zone expansion playbook** (Pre-Launch → Launch Day → Stabilization → Ongoing) is practical and repeatable.
- **"The Delivered Collection" product naming** (The Commuter, The Night Shift, The Weekender) is creative and consumer-friendly.
- **Onboarding flow design** shows genuine UX thinking.

## Recommendations (Specific Changes Needed)

### For Plan 1 (Business Case + Deal)
1. **Add license expiration acknowledgment.** Make renewal confirmation a precondition.
2. **Remove Apple Pay reference.** Replace with "streamlined ACH/debit checkout."
3. **Present three revenue scenarios** (conservative/base/optimistic) with sensitivity analysis.
4. **Lower the baseline revenue estimate** or present it as a range ($35K–$65K) and ask Delivered to confirm.
5. **Add "Why Not Just Hire a Dev Shop" section** that directly addresses the equity objection.
6. **Lead with the hack cleanup** as proof of immediate value before discussing equity.
7. **Restructure the AC relationship** as "facilitated introduction" unless there's a signed commitment.
8. **Add clear IP ownership terms** as a standalone section, not buried in dissolution protections.

### For Plan 2 (Technical Spec)
1. **Extend the timeline to 14–18 weeks** or dramatically reduce MVP scope.
2. **Add Apple App Store approval risk** and mitigation strategy.
3. **Make this document less like an internal spec** and more like a partner-facing capability demonstration. Founders don't care about database schemas — they care about what the user sees and feels.
4. **Add driver app scope.** Real-time tracking requires a companion driver app. This is never mentioned.
5. **Address Dutchie API integration risk.** Dutchie may not offer open API access, or may restrict it for cannabis delivery. This is a real technical dependency that's assumed but never validated.

### For Plan 3 (Creative + Brand)
1. **Add MA advertising compliance overlay** to all proposed tactics.
2. **Remove billboard concept** or reframe as compliant alternatives.
3. **Unify the document voice** — it reads like two separate drafts stitched together.
4. **Ground the Instagram growth targets** (1.1K → 10K in 12 months is possible but needs budget — organic-only won't get there in cannabis where reach is algorithmically suppressed).
5. **Add budget estimates** for the creative execution. Photography, video series, podcast production, multilingual content, PR kits — none of this is free, and none of it is costed.
6. **Tone down the lifestyle consumption imagery** — MA regs prohibit depicting cannabis consumption in advertising.

### For All Plans
1. **Create a single, unified executive summary** that references all three plans. Currently they feel like three separate pitches from three different agencies.
2. **Add a "What Delivered Brings" section.** The plans exhaustively cover what Roviq brings but never acknowledge what Delivered's founders have built. This feels one-sided and may come across as dismissive of their 2.5 years of work.
3. **Add a simple one-page term sheet** that summarizes the recommended deal. Founders shouldn't have to read 30+ pages to understand what they're being asked to agree to.

### Most Damaging Conflict
Plan 1 correctly labels this as a **Seed round** ($1–3M range) but also mentions a "$2–5M seed round" later. Plan 2 calls it a **Series A** deck outline. A cannabis delivery company doing $2M ARR in a $20M market is a Seed-stage company, not Series A. This inconsistency makes us look like we don't understand fundraising stages. **Fix:** Standardize on "Seed Round" across all plans.

## Final Verdict

**These plans are 70% of the way there.** The market thesis is strong, the app gap is real, and the social equity moat is genuinely compelling. But the plans are too long, contain factual errors (Apple Pay), miss critical issues (license expiration, MA advertising law), present overly optimistic timelines (8–10 week app build), and haven't been reconciled with each other (conflicting numbers, tiers, fundraising stages).

**The biggest strategic risk is tone.** These plans tell Delivered what Roviq can do for them. They don't adequately acknowledge what Delivered has already built, or address the fundamental question every founder asks when someone wants equity: **"Why should I give away part of my company when I could just pay for these services?"**

Before presenting, the plans need:
1. License expiration addressed
2. Apple Pay removed
3. Revenue projections grounded with scenarios
4. App timeline extended to realistic range
5. MA advertising compliance added
6. Numbers reconciled across all three documents
7. A one-page term sheet summary
8. A genuine "What Delivered Brings" acknowledgment

**Ship these fixes, and this becomes a strong pitch. Ship them as-is, and a savvy founder will see the gaps before page 5.**
