## A Neighborhood Built by Immigrants

East Boston wasn't always part of Boston. It was created in the 1830s when developers connected five small islands in the harbor — Noddle's Island, Hog Island, Governor's Island, Apple Island, and Bird Island — filling in the channels between them to form a single landmass. By the 1840s, it had become one of Boston's major shipbuilding hubs, and the first waves of immigrants started arriving: Canadians, then Irish families fleeing the famine.

By the late 1800s, Eastern European Jews and Italians made Eastie their home. The Italian influence ran deep — Santarpio's bakery (now the famous pizza joint) opened in 1903 and is still run by the family. By 1915, East Boston was majority Italian, and you can still see that heritage in the architecture, the street names, and the way people gather around food.

But the biggest chapter in Eastie's immigration story started in the 1980s, when Central American families — especially from El Salvador, Colombia, and Guatemala — began settling here in larger numbers. Many were fleeing civil wars and violence. They found community in the same triple-deckers and neighborhood squares that had welcomed generations before them.

Today, more than half of East Boston's roughly 47,000 residents identify as Latino or Hispanic. Walk down Meridian Street or through Maverick Square and you'll hear Spanish as often as English. The Veronica Robles Cultural Center, housed in the former East Boston Library on Meridian Street, serves as a hub for Latino cultural events. Colombian bakeries sit next to Italian delis. Salvadoran pupuserias share blocks with Australian meat pie shops.

That layering of cultures — Italian, Colombian, Salvadoran, Guatemalan, and more — is what makes Eastie unlike any other neighborhood in Boston.

## What Makes Eastie Worth Your Time

### The Food

Honestly, the food alone is reason enough to spend an afternoon here. A handful of the spots we recommend to anyone who asks:

**Santarpio's Pizza** on Chelsea Street has been a Boston institution for over a century. Cash only, no frills, just some of the best pizza in the city. You can actually see their sign from the airport runway. Get the barbecue lamb tips while you're at it — it's the order that regulars know about.

**Angela's Cafe** on Meridian Street serves Colombian and Latin American dishes that feel like they were made in someone's abuela's kitchen. Generous portions, warm atmosphere, and one of the places that made Eastie's food scene famous beyond the neighborhood.

**Rincón Limeño** is an authentic Peruvian restaurant near Maverick Square — dark and cozy inside, with a full menu of ceviches, arroz con pollo, and seco de cabrito (a Peruvian-style lamb stew that's worth a detour). Outdoor patio seating when the weather cooperates.

**Taqueria Jalisco** for street-style Mexican tacos. Nothing fancy, everything delicious.

**KO Pies** is the wildcard — an Australian-style meat pie shop that somehow fits perfectly into Eastie's international food mix. Try the curry pie.

**The Tall Ship** is a 245-foot vessel moored at Pier One that's been converted into a floating oyster bar. Live entertainment, food trucks, popup events, and views of the Boston skyline from the water. It's the kind of place that doesn't exist anywhere else in the city.

**Maverick House Tavern** at 154 Maverick Street is good for a drink after dinner — trivia nights on Tuesdays, open mic on Wednesdays, and a laid-back neighborhood bar vibe that's becoming harder to find in Boston.

### The Waterfront

East Boston has some of the best waterfront access in the entire city, and most Bostonians don't even know it.

**Piers Park** is the crown jewel. Eleven acres of waterfront parkland with sweeping views of the Boston skyline, a 600-foot boardwalk, playground, fitness area, and the Piers Park Sailing Center where you can actually get out on the water. When Prince William and Kate Middleton visited Boston in 2023, they came here. It's a ten-minute walk from Maverick Station.

**Constitution Beach** — locals call it Shays Beach — is up near Orient Heights. It's not the fanciest beach you'll ever see, but it has something no other beach in Massachusetts offers: a front-row seat to planes taking off and landing at Logan. Kids love it. Photographers love it. There's a seasonal snack bar and a playground, plus an indoor skating rink nearby.

**The Mary Ellen Welch Greenway** is a 3.3-mile pathway that stretches from Jeffries Point all the way to Constitution Beach. Running, biking, walking the dog — it's Eastie's version of a linear park, built along an abandoned railroad corridor.

**LoPresti Park** sits on the waterfront near Maverick with basketball courts, a hockey rink, artificial turf soccer field, and waterfront fishing. Walk along the pier and you'll hit the Skyline Monument with one of the best photo angles of downtown Boston you'll find.

**Bremen Street Park** separates the residential part of East Boston from the airport — a buffer zone that Massport developed as part of the Big Dig project. It's a quiet spot with a dog park, community garden, and a greenway path that runs through it.

### Getting Around

East Boston is better connected than most people realize.

The **MBTA Blue Line** runs through the neighborhood with stations at Maverick, Airport, Wood Island, Orient Heights, and Suffolk Downs. Maverick to downtown Boston (Aquarium station) is literally one stop — a few minutes through an underwater tunnel. That makes Eastie one of the most conveniently located neighborhoods in the city for commuters and visitors.

The **East Boston ferry** runs to the Seaport District for about $5. It's a short, scenic ride across the harbor with views you'd pay a lot more for on a tourist boat.

By car, Route 1A (McClellan Highway) runs through the neighborhood connecting to the airport, the Tobin Bridge, and Route 1 north to Revere and the North Shore. The Sumner and Callahan Tunnels connect directly to downtown.

## The Changing Face of Eastie

We'd be leaving out an important part of the story if we didn't mention this: East Boston is changing. New apartment buildings are going up along the waterfront. Rents have climbed. Some longtime families — many of them the same Latino families who built this neighborhood's culture — have been pushed to Revere, Lynn, and other communities further out.

That tension between growth and preservation is real, and it matters to us. We believe that businesses operating in Eastie have a responsibility to support the people who've been here, not just the people moving in. That means fair employment, community involvement, and respect for the neighborhood's identity.

Cannabis Healing isn't just trying to sell products in East Boston. We're trying to be part of East Boston.
